# What the studio should sell first

Start with the **Menu sales mix review** if a restaurant owner can supply four weeks of item sales and has a menu decision coming up. It uses the partner’s analytics experience, gives the owner something to act on, and can lead to a well-briefed design job. For a brand preparing a buyer meeting, lead with the **Wholesale line sheet**. For a service business whose staff repeatedly explain the same packages, lead with the **Service tier comparison sheet**. Pick the first pilot by the buyer you can reach, the material they already have, and the date they will use the result.

## Three pilot offers

### 1. Menu sales mix review

**What to sell.** “Which two menu items deserve a test on your next menu?” For one location and up to 40 items, deliver a workbook showing each item’s share of units and net sales, plus a one-page brief naming two items and a test for each. Ask the owner which decision is open, such as a featured lunch item or the use of a small space on the counter.

**What you need.** Request four complete weeks of item-level totals, the location and date filters, refunds, and any stockouts, closures, promotions or renamed items. Item totals are sufficient for this pilot. Square’s item report supports unit counts, gross and net sales, date and location filters, and export. Renamed items can appear as separate entries, so have the owner confirm which rows describe the same item. [Square item sales documentation](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8363-view-item-category-and-modifiers-sales-reports)

**What makes it useful.** Reconcile the workbook to the source report for the same period. Show the denominator for each percentage. An item with a high share of units and a low share of sales merits a conversation about its role and price. Ask about portion size, availability and ingredient cost before suggesting a change. Ingredient costing, margin estimates and a forecast each require additional inputs and a separate scope.

**When to sell the next job.** If the owner chooses an item to feature, offer a **Menu refresh · print-ready** or a **Table-tent promo · 100** for that test. Agree in advance on the next comparable period and the measure to watch. Offer a **Monthly sales decision brief** when the owner can provide consistent exports and names a new decision. Record promotions or closures alongside the observed change.

### 2. Wholesale line sheet

**What to sell.** “Can a buyer place an order for this collection from one PDF?” For a brand approaching retailers, deliver a four-page wholesale PDF covering up to 12 items and an editable table linking each item to its photo, order code, wholesale price and case quantity.

**What you need.** Ask for the intended buyer, meeting or buying deadline, product photos, variant codes, approved wholesale terms, minimums and order contact. A Shopify merchant can export selected products or a filtered collection. Use the export to match products and variants, then ask the brand to approve its wholesale prices and pack sizes. Keep the working file separate from the merchant’s store. [Shopify product exports](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/import-export/export-products) · [Shopify product CSV fields](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/import-export/using-csv)

**What makes it useful.** Hand a proof to someone unfamiliar with the range. Ask them to choose one variant, find its case quantity, calculate the value of a case and identify how to order. Correct any ambiguity against the brand’s approved table. Quote buyer outreach, new photography, price strategy and order processing separately.

**When to sell the next job.** If the codes and images cannot be matched, sell **Product variant cleanup** first. If the brand has a confirmed date for a new collection, offer a **Seasonal assortment lookbook** for the new range. A revised price or the addition of two products may only need a small line-sheet update.

### 3. Service tier comparison sheet

**What to sell.** “What should a customer book, and what is included in that price?” For a salon, repair shop, maintenance provider or other local service business, turn three approved tiers of one service into a one-page PDF, an editable copy and three short booking prompts.

**What you need.** Ask the owner for the three prices, inclusions, boundaries, booking route and the question staff hear most. Ask a staff member to walk through one recent inquiry. If the team gives different answers about the tiers, the owner needs to settle those differences before layout.

**What makes it useful.** Have a staff member use the proof to answer three example inquiries. For each one, check that the customer can identify the appropriate tier, what costs extra and the next booking step. The client approves its prices and service commitments. Contract drafting, website changes and booking-system work have separate scopes.

**When to sell the next job.** A client who still fields preparation questions after booking is a candidate for a **First-visit welcome pack**. Revisit the comparison sheet when the owner changes an inclusion, a price or a booking path. A standing monthly refresh makes sense only when the underlying offer changes that often.

## How to combine three offers for one buyer

In **Offers → Choose a client**, start with a buyer whose decision you understand. Each recommended service has a separate job. The client can buy each independently. The estimate models inquiries for the individual offers and uses your existing demand assumptions.

| Client | Suggested menu | When I would swap an offer |
|---|---|---|
| Independent restaurant | Menu sales mix review → Menu refresh → Table-tent promotion | Keep the analysis and refreshed menu. Use a digital flyer for a pickup promotion when the owner can share an ordering link. |
| Bilingual café | Bilingual ordering guide → Counter screen menu loop → Seasonal preorder kit | Choose the placement the café actually controls. A table tent suits seated ordering, and a screen loop needs a compatible display. |
| Wholesale product brand | Product variant cleanup → Product image standardization → Wholesale line sheet | A buyer meeting calls for a compact, orderable range. A seasonal lookbook suits a collection with approved images and a dated launch. |
| Neighborhood retailer | Product margin coverage check → Window promotion → Social promotion pack | Start with discount analysis if the owner’s decision is whether to repeat a discount and they can supply the sales and discount records. |
| Maker sourcing packaging | Packaging quote request pack → Supplier quote comparison → Supplier sample review brief | Use a bilingual care insert in the final role when the buyer has approved the packaging and can supply final instructions in both languages. |
| Appointment-based studio | Appointment capacity review → Service tier comparison → First-visit welcome pack | Bilingual appointment messages make sense when clients need instructions in both languages and the studio has approved wording. |
| Home service business | Completed project case study → Seasonal maintenance reminder → Appointment capacity review | A comparison sheet can answer questions about service levels when the customer already knows the company and is deciding what to book. |

Try one swap at a time. Look at both the change in operating result and the extra hours it would take to earn it. A recurring offer in this preview contributes first orders. The simulation handles later quotes, delays and paid work over time. Use **Apply menu** to keep the combination and **Adjust client menu** to revisit it.

## Quote, deliver and decide whether to repeat

1. **Qualify the decision.** Ask what the buyer needs to do, by when, and what they can send today. Inspect a small sample. If essential inputs are missing, quote the preparation work on its own or defer the pilot.
2. **Write a fixed scope.** Name the source, quantity, output format, approver, one included feedback round for these three pilots, and the fee for extra scope. Put the delivery clock against receipt of approved inputs. Assign analytical or production work to the person who can do it and leave time for the director’s review.
3. **Cost the real work.** Estimate production labor at its paid rate, director time for quoting, coordination and delivery, and any supplier or freight charge. At handoff, record the actual hours, feedback rounds, cash paid out and payment date. Track contribution after direct costs, then the amount left after overhead and the value of the director’s time.
4. **Ask what the buyer did.** Did the restaurant choose an item to test, the wholesale buyer use the order codes, or staff use the sheet during bookings? Record the answer and the date of the next decision. A customer agreeing to a second paid job is stronger evidence of repeat demand than an internal calendar entry.

## Use the simulator to test the menu

The catalog seeds these **illustrative inputs** for one engagement. Replace them with a quote and measured hours as soon as you have them.

| Pilot | Model customer fee | Model production hours | Model owner review hours |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Menu sales mix review | $425 | 6 | 1.5 |
| Wholesale line sheet | $575 | 9 | 1.5 |
| Service tier comparison sheet | $335 | 5 | 1.25 |

Start an **Offers** menu with the pilot you can actually sell. Add a second when it serves buyers you expect to reach. Set inquiry shares from the mix of requests you expect among eligible customers. Set total incoming inquiries and quote acceptance in **Demand**. Treat the supplied fees, customer mix and conversion as scenarios until you have client evidence.

In **People**, use designer hours for the person doing the modeled production work, including analysis or catalog preparation where that is a reasonable approximation. Owner review hours cover only part of the director’s time. The simulator also accounts for activities such as outreach, quoting and delivery. A specialist with a different hourly rate or an independent queue needs a separate cost estimate. In **Cash & timing**, enter deposit, final-payment terms and overhead. A profitable job can still leave a balance to collect after delivery.

Run the menu for a period that matches the decision. If design work queues up, change available production hours and simulate again with the same workload seed and duration. If cash is the concern, change payment terms and compare closing cash and receivables as well as operating result. Keep the setup you would quote from and revisit the saved comparison when real inquiries, hours or payments arrive.

The setup estimate assumes expected first orders earn their price during the period. The run can include delayed deliveries, unpaid balances and later paid work. A 30- or 90-day campaign cadence creates an opportunity for another quote. Use confirmed orders and paid invoices to decide what belongs on the studio’s real menu.
